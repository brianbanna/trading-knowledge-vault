# Shadow Fleet

## Definition

The shadow fleet is a large pool of aging tankers used to move oil that the mainstream shipping and insurance system will not touch. Think of it as a parallel logistics network for barrels that are sanctioned or price capped. The vessels share a common profile: opaque ownership hidden behind shell companies, obscure or absent Western marine insurance, gaps in their AIS transmissions (going dark so they cannot be tracked), and frequent flag hopping between flag registries of convenience.

More precisely, it is the fleet that carries [[Urals Crude]] and other Russian, Iranian, and Venezuelan barrels outside the G7 price cap and outside the mainstream protection and indemnity (P and I) insurance clubs. When a cargo would breach the cap or trigger a sanctions violation for a Western owner, insurer, or financier, it moves on a shadow vessel instead. The fleet exists precisely to break the link between a barrel and the Western service providers who would otherwise refuse to handle it.

Context from the tape: the EU is tightening action against shadow fleet tankers falsely flying [[Cameroon]]'s flag, including boarding vessels at sea. That is a direct sanctions to [[Freight]] transmission. When enforcement rises, sanctioned barrels get harder to lift, discounts on those grades widen, and demand shifts back toward mainstream tankers that can prove clean insurance and ownership.

## Why it matters (commodities and FX)

The shadow fleet is the mechanism that decides whether sanctions actually remove barrels from the market or just reroute them. If the fleet is large and enforcement is loose, sanctioned crude keeps flowing at a discount and the global balance barely tightens. If enforcement bites, the flow chokes, discounts on capped grades widen, and mainstream freight rates rise as buyers scramble for compliant tonnage. Every sanctions headline has to be read through the fleet's capacity to absorb it.

For a crude trader this is a [[Location Arbitrage]] and grade differential story. Sanctioned barrels trade at a discount to [[Brent Crude]] that reflects the cost and risk of moving them on shadow tonnage. Widen the enforcement risk and you widen the discount. For freight it is a demand signal: every barrel that shifts off shadow tankers and back onto mainstream tankers tightens the clean tanker market and lifts [[Voyage Charter]] rates. For FX it feeds the oil revenue of sanctioned exporters, which supports or pressures their currencies and fiscal positions.

The fleet also concentrates safety and [[Chokepoint]] risk. Aging, uninsured vessels transiting the [[Strait of Hormuz]] or the Danish straits raise the probability of an accident with no underwriter behind it, which is exactly the kind of event that spikes a [[War Risk Premium]] and forces reroutes.

## Concrete example

**Concrete:** A trader watches Urals sell into India at a 12 USD per barrel discount to [[Brent Crude]] while Brent sits at 80 USD. That 12 USD discount is the shadow fleet toll: extra freight on aging tonnage, a premium for insurers willing to cover sanctioned cargo, and the sanctions risk the buyer demands to be paid for. Then the EU starts boarding tankers falsely flying [[Cameroon]]'s flag and delists 60 vessels. Available shadow tonnage drops, so the remaining ships charge more and some buyers refuse the cargo entirely. The Urals discount widens from 12 to 20 USD as sellers cut price to clear barrels, and mainstream [[Voyage Charter]] rates on clean tankers jump because the marginal buyer now wants a compliant ship. The counter case: enforcement is announced but never carried out, the fleet keeps operating, the discount drifts back to 10 USD, and the mainstream freight spike fades within weeks. The trade is in the enforcement credibility, not the headline.

**Simplified:** Sanctioned oil rides a fleet of old, anonymous, uninsured tankers because normal shipping will not carry it. That underground fleet charges a toll, and the toll shows up as a discount on the sanctioned barrel. Crack down on the fleet and the toll rises, the discount widens, and normal tankers get busier. Do nothing and the oil keeps flowing at roughly the same discount.

## Key mechanics and formulas

The sanctioned grade discount is roughly the sum of the extra costs of using shadow tonnage:

`Discount to benchmark ≈ extra freight + insurance premium + sanctions risk compensation + buyer bargaining margin`

Enforcement works on the first three terms. Boarding vessels, delisting ships, and sanctioning insurers all raise the cost or cut the supply of shadow tonnage, which widens the discount and pushes marginal barrels back toward mainstream freight.

Mainstream tanker demand response:

`Δ mainstream tanker demand ≈ (barrels displaced from shadow fleet) / (voyage tonne-miles per vessel)`

The signatures that mark a vessel as shadow fleet:
- Ownership behind layered shell companies in opaque jurisdictions
- No mainstream P and I club cover, or cover from an unrecognized provider
- AIS gaps (going dark) around load and discharge, often with spoofed positions
- Flag hopping between registries of convenience, sometimes flags the state itself disowns
- Age well beyond the 15 year mainstream scrapping norm

## Common misconceptions

**"Sanctions stop the oil."** Usually they reroute it. The shadow fleet exists to keep sanctioned barrels flowing to non aligned buyers at a discount. Volumes often hold up while the discount and the freight cost adjust.

**"Shadow fleet vessels are a small fringe."** Estimates run to hundreds of tankers moving a meaningful share of Russian and Iranian exports. It is a structural parallel market, not a handful of rogue ships.

**"Going dark on AIS proves illegal activity."** AIS gaps are a strong signal but not proof. Vessels also go dark for legitimate safety reasons. The fleet signature is the cluster of traits together: ownership, insurance, AIS, flag, and age.

**"Enforcement instantly clears the market."** Boarding and delisting raise costs at the margin but the fleet adapts with new shells, new flags, and reflagging. The price effect is real but often partial and temporary unless enforcement is sustained.
