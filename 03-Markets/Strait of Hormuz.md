# Strait of Hormuz

## Definition

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway between Iran and Oman connecting the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and the open ocean. ~33 km wide at the narrowest point, with shipping lanes only ~3 km wide each direction. Roughly 20% of global oil supply (~21 million barrels/day) and ~25% of global LNG trade transit it. It is the single most important chokepoint in global energy markets.

## Why it matters (commodities and FX)

Any disruption, actual or threatened, causes immediate spikes in [[Brent Crude]], [[WTI]], and [[LNG]] prices. The strait is the supply chain bottleneck for Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Kuwait, UAE, and Qatar. Energy traders monitor regional geopolitical tension constantly. Even a credible closure threat (Iranian military exercises, tanker seizures) adds $5 to $15/barrel of risk premium to crude.

## Concrete example

**Concrete:** Iran threatens to close the strait following new sanctions. [[Brent Crude]] at $78/barrel. Iran's Revolutionary Guard announces naval exercises near the strait and threatens consequences if sanctions tighten. Trader buys 10 Brent contracts at $78. Over 3 days the risk premium pushes Brent to $86. Trader sells at $85. Gain: $7 × 1,000 bbl × 10 = $70,000.

**Simplified:** A fifth of the world's oil travels through a strip of water 33 km wide between Iran and Oman. Every time Iran rattles a saber or seizes a tanker, traders price in a small probability that the strait closes. Crude jumps $5 to $15 within hours. If the threat fades, the premium evaporates. Trading Hormuz is binary and fast: get in quickly on credible threats, exit quickly when tensions cool, do not hold through resolution.

## Reading a Hormuz event

A Hormuz headline is not a disruption. The desk job is to separate a scary headline from a barrel that actually failed to move. Run this playbook before adding or holding risk. The full timing model behind it is in [[Geopolitical Repricing Sequence]].

**Headline risk versus flow confirmed.** Two columns. The left column fades; the right column is believed.

| Headline risk signature (fade it) | Flow confirmed signature (believe it) |
| --- | --- |
| "Strait closed" rhetoric, naval exercises, inspections, tanker harassment | A confirmed tanker hit, mine, or seized cargo that fails to deliver |
| Implied vol and call skew spike on the headline alone | Transit counts fall toward zero, tankers divert and turn back |
| Flat [[Brent Crude]] gaps up, then fades within days | War risk insurance on Gulf voyages reprices 4 to 6x, underwriter capital behind it |
| Move is broad, [[Brent Crude]] and [[WTI Crude Oil]] rally together | Sour Gulf grades lead, [[Brent-WTI Spread]] blows out |
| A single print with no corroboration | The five signatures fire together (see checklist) |

**Confirmation checklist (the five signature cluster).** One print is noise. You want these firing together. If [[Brent Crude]] rallies but the cluster does not follow, the move is something global wearing a Hormuz costume.

- [ ] [[Brent Crude]] rallies (seaborne benchmark, most directly exposed)
- [ ] [[WTI Crude Oil]] lags and the [[Brent-WTI Spread]] widens (Cushing is landlocked, not Hormuz exposed)
- [ ] Sour Gulf grades ([[Dubai Crude]], Oman) rally hardest (the barrels physically at risk)
- [ ] Asian refining margins compress (Asia is the largest buyer of Gulf crude)
- [ ] Gulf freight and war risk insurance jump (the physical leg repricing real danger)

**Signal hierarchy: vol < insurance < transit counts.** Three gauges, ranked by how close each sits to the barrel.

- **Implied vol and skew (rumor).** Measure fear. Can stay elevated on pure sentiment with no barrel at risk. Earliest signal, weakest evidence. Vol is the question the market is asking, not the answer.
- **War risk insurance (assessed danger).** Underwriters price real payout risk to specific vessels on specific routes, their own capital behind the quote. Harder than vol because someone wears the loss, but underwriters panic too, so it can overshoot.
- **Transit counts (verdict).** CENTCOM, Kpler, Vortexa, TankerTrackers. Did a barrel fail to move. This is the only question that settles it. When vol and flow disagree, flow wins.

**Live read, 2026 episode (28 Feb to 17 June ceasefire).** Brent ran from the high $70s to a $126 peak in March, then shed close to 40% as the flat premium collapsed the day the ceasefire was signed. On 20 June Iran declared the strait "closed", yet CENTCOM logged 55 ships and 17 million barrels the next day. No barrel stopped, so Brent fell from $80.57 toward $77. The market faded the bluff. The late, crowded trade was flat Brent at $126; the edge was being positioned across the curve and vol surface before 28 Feb and unwinding into the ceasefire.

## Key mechanics and formulas

**Key statistics:**
- Width at narrowest: ~33 km (21 miles)
- Shipping lane width: ~3 km each direction
- Daily oil flow: ~21 million bbl/day (~20% of global supply)
- Daily LNG flow: ~25% of global LNG trade
- Key exporters transiting: Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Kuwait, UAE, Qatar

**Risk premium estimation:**

Crude risk premium = probability_of_disruption × expected_price_impact × expected_duration

Example: 10% chance of a 2 week disruption causing a $30/bbl spike = $30 × 0.10 = $3/bbl embedded risk premium

**Alternative routes (and limits):**
- East West Pipeline (Saudi): 5M bbl/day capacity, bypasses Hormuz
- Abu Dhabi ADCOP pipeline: 1.5M bbl/day, bypasses Hormuz
- Total alternative capacity: ~6.5M bbl/day vs 21M bbl/day transiting, leaving ~14.5M bbl/day at risk

## Common misconceptions

**"Closing the strait would only affect oil."** Qatar is the world's largest LNG exporter and all its cargoes transit Hormuz. A closure spikes [[LNG]] globally with cascading effects on European and Asian power markets.

**"Iran would never actually close it."** Iran's own oil exports transit the strait, so full closure is self destructive. Partial disruption (harassing tankers, mining, seizing vessels) is entirely plausible and has happened (Tanker War 1984 to 1988, 2019 tanker seizures).

**"The price spike from a disruption is permanent."** Historically, Hormuz spikes reverse quickly once the threat subsides. Trade these with fast entry and disciplined exit, not buy and hold.
